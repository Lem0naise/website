* signifies a comment
St Teire

“Have you lost the entirety of your senses, or just the one which keeps your head upon your shoulders?”
Very few people could speak to the King of Kresimira in terms like these and expect to see another sunrise. My mother, of course, had never considered herself ‘people’. She felt no deference to crown or robe - least of all when worn by her son.
“The people will not abide by this, Remus. The priests will demand fire and blood. And the Mannfords?” Her voice dropped. “They are already sharpening their daggers. Must I spell it out for you, Remus?”
I had, of course, braced myself for her inevitable disapproval. What I had not anticipated was this tempest of fury - threaded with veiled warnings of the impending blade that may find me in the dark.
~

The wooden thud of the House Toran gavel sent a dulled echo throughout the panelled chambers. At long last, the decision had been made. Whether or not the feeling of success would last was another matter entirely. Silence hung in the air for a brief moment, before giving way to a rising tide of bustling chatter from the benches below. 
“Your Majesty!”
The wooden door to my right burst open. Milan stood in the frame, breathless, eyes alight with excitement - not at all with the usual decorum my butler was taught to carry himself with, but with something rare. Unrestrained happiness.
“Thank you, Your Majesty,” he said in his faint Eastern accent as he took steps towards me. 
I gave him a gentle, measured nod in reply.
“There is still much to be done, Milan,” I said quietly. “A decree is but ink on parchment - it does not line the streets of Sinj, nor does it soften the hearts of the nobles who smile while they reach for their daggers. You know as well as I, Milan: the nobility plays their hand at courtesy while they plot feverishly behind closed doors.“
“Of course, Your Majesty. There always remains more work to be done.” 
He returned my nod, smile slowly wearing off, his signature serious look returning. I waved him away kindly as he bowed again, and quickly left the chamber.
Milan was right. The majority of the Kresimirian people were still traditionalists, Teirists at heart, the older citizens especially. They knelt to St Teire every morning, whispered scripture under their breath, and cast wary glances at change, as if it were something that could spread like fire. Change was coming slowly, as it always would be - but had I forced it too soon? The internal battle the law had provoked in the first place had been brutal, but the uphill war to retain it had just begun. 
I took a deep breath and slowly sighed, feeling the soft leather armrests under the weight of the decisions swimming in my mind. But I was confident I could make it work. The problem was convincing everybody else of that. Persuading the people would be slow, but not impossible. The people are malleable, as my father had always said, if you put your mind to it - and there was nowhere my mind was more focused on. The nobles on the other hand - God. Certain nobles would no doubt lose their minds - in a respectful manner. I hope. After all, my decision is, by all technicalities, entirely final - even Teodor Mannford, for all his flaws, knows the House is ceremonial. 
Not to mention my Council. Vihrdecker would rage, as he always did, convinced the crown of Kresimira sat just as heavily on his head as it did mine. That, however, was a battle for tomorrow. 
And then there was Mother. 

~

“Tell me, Remus - have you forgotten the basic understanding of how to rule a kingdom?”
The evening air was thick with the sound of her voice - sharp, clipped, unyielding. Outside, the songbirds chirped, oblivious. Inside, her words pecked at me with equal force. In all honesty, I’d had less conflict with the delegate from the Alandir Confederacy the week before last. At least they hadn’t shouted and spat at me in my own meeting room. 
“Remus, are you listening?”
Of course I wasn’t listening. Engaging her would be pointless - but ignoring her would be worse.
“Yes, Mother,” I said.
“What on earth were you thinking, Remus? Sinners? In the Holy Kingdom of Kresimiria?” 
She let the silence hang with a firm, measured shake of her head. 
“St Teire weeps for you, Remus. Your father would have wept, too. You’re making an absolute mockery of our religion, Remus. They best not make themselves known in Sinj Cathedral, Remus, I swear to you. I’d flee the country for Kaskiv before I attend a Teirist service in the Cathedral with one of… them.” She spat the last word out with a certain revulsion as if a mouthful of rotten fish. I stared at her, in thinly veiled disgust at the vile things coming out of my own mother's mouth. “Milan,” I called out after a moment, and the door behind us drew open, my butler entering purposefully. 
“Milan,” I said, without taking my eyes off her. “See that my mother is comfortably settled for the night.” 
She stiffened. I knew she hated nothing more than being ‘handled’ like a delicate thing. Good. Milan approaching to touch her shoulder was immediately shot down with a fiercely indignant look.
“Milan, leave,” she said firmly, without a hint of uncertainty. Milan looked waveringly at me, as if waiting for permission. I glanced at him, and back at her, into her dark eyes, watching for a reaction. It was clear there was no satisfying her until she was finished. 
“Leave us,” I sighed, and he quickly obliged.
Mother, bless her soul, had the compassion of a stone gargoyle. I doubted it was worth embracing the empathetic angle here, but I nonetheless tried. Maybe a religious speech could stir up her empathy.
“Mother, tell me. Are we Kresimirians judged by our faith or by our nature? You speak of sin - ‘who among us is without it?’” 
Her fists struck the table with a weighty thud, the candle flames flickering. I didn’t react. I knew quoting St Teire would provoke her, it was meant to. She leant forward, fiery eyes locking onto mine, daring me to flinch. I knew what was coming next. It always would, after an argument, something I did, some law I passed, she would always compare me to - 
“You’ll never be your father, you know that, Remus.”
I’d become accustomed to this over the years. That didn’t stop the weight of it crushing my heart in my chest, though. 
“Eder, may St Teire bless his soul, would never have stood for this. You try to wear his crown, his robes, sit in his throne, carry his name - but you are nothing like your father. Nothing gives you the right to disrespect the blessed name of House Toran like this - ”
I pushed my chair backwards abruptly, the hand-carved legs scraping against the wooden floor.
“I will take your views into consideration, Mother,” I interrupted. “I always do. As I did my father’s.”
She looked up at me sharply, her tirade interrupted unexpectedly. Her mouth tightened, the displeasure of my behaviour flashing across her eyes. A flicker of something else crossed her face - too fast to catch.
“Your father…” Her voice wavered. She exhaled sharply, as if trying to steady herself. “He would have known what to do in situations such as these.”
She turned her head away, rubbing at the bridge of her nose with two fingers. The deep breath she took barely disguised the uneven hitch in her throat.
“It shouldn’t have been you,” she whispered to herself. The words were barely audible. And then, a shuddering breath. Her shoulders trembled. “It should have been me.”
I hesitated, then stepped closer, laying a careful hand on her arm, the black silk soft beneath my fingers. She flinched - just slightly - but enough for me to feel the rejection. 
“Don’t,” she murmured, shaking her head. “Don’t touch me.” She wiped at her eyes with a laced handkerchief, quick and furious, as if she could erase the tears before they could exist.
Just as I was about to step away, her hand shot out, grabbing my wrist. And then, before I could process anything, she pulled me into a tight, breathless embrace. 
“You think you understand everything, Remus,” she murmured against my shoulder, her voice shaking with tears. “But you don’t, you don’t.” Her grip was ironclad around my waist, refusing to let me go. 
“You don’t,” she repeated. “You don't. He would.”
When she finally pulled away, she didn’t look at me. She lifted the handkerchief from her lap and wiped her face briskly, dabbing the corners of her mouth, as if to seal her lips. Then a quick nod and a sharp inhale, as if reassembling herself piece by piece. 
“Good night, Remus.” And then, she was gone.

I let myself fall back into my chair, head spinning and heart beating, letting the tide of my breath calm. 
“Milan?” I called out again, waiting for Mother to be out of earshot. 
The door opened, and Milan quickly stepped inside and closed it after him. He nodded swiftly to me, signalling nobody was listening. I’d told Gustav, the Chief of the King’s Guard, that when Mother left the room I wanted time alone. 
Alone with Milan, that was.

~

“What is it you need, Your Majesty?”
I tilted my head slightly, my gaze catching on the crisp fold of Milan’s cuff, his sleeve barely brushing his wrist. His suit, as always, was immaculate - tailored by none other than the famed Kresimirian stylist, Doria de Virell. It was a testament to his patience, his precision - not a thread out of place. It never was. 
His matching bow tie sat perfectly on his wing collar, not an inch outsized or a degree off centre. White shirt pressed and steamed to perfection. As I gazed, he relaxed his usual stiff posture, and let out an understanding sigh. 
The hazy evening sun spilled through the bay windows, basking even the faintest shadow on his jawline in a soft, warm glow. The last of the evening light caught in the strands of his hair, turning brown to gold. Perhaps it was the lighting, or perhaps it was the events of the week hanging in my mind - but his softly manicured hair, his smooth, clean-shaven cheeks, and the tantalising suggestion of a smile - I couldn’t think straight.
He looked… I exhaled, shifting in my seat. It didn’t matter. I looked away before my gaze lingered too long.
I hesitated. “Milan.” My voice was quieter than I intended. He looked up immediately, as if he had been waiting for it. 
“Remus…” he whispered back. He didn’t finish the sentence. Just my name, as if it was the answer to something.
He looked at me, a faint smile now on his face, his chestnut eyes dreamy and expectant. 
“This -” I gestured vaguely, then let my hand drop. “You know we can’t. Not… not yet.” I frustrated myself sometimes with the soundness of my judgment.
He nodded slowly.
“I know,” he murmured, glancing away. Then, quieter: “but there is hope, for us?” His hand twitched against his side, fingers briefly curling into a fist before relaxing again.
“There is.” The faint smile had disappeared from his lips, but I saw the hope still shimmering in his eyes.
“I need a son, Milan,” I said slowly, choosing my words carefully. “And the only person who can give that son to me is Lyssa.”
He surely knew I couldn't make more waves, not now. Not with the state of the country over the past couple of months. If there was anyone who had to remain on my side, it was the nobles.
Milan’s lips parted as if to protest, but he sighed instead, his shoulders sinking. 
“I know, Remus.”
His eyes watched me closely, following mine as I looked across the room at the gradually vanishing sunset. I didn’t quite know what else to say. It was Milan, after all. Milan knew exactly what I was thinking, even if I didn’t know quite how to say it.
He sighed again, in deep thought.
“Soon, Remus.” Then, almost to himself: “Please, soon.”
I nodded again. “Soon.”
Milan gave a small instinctive bow, and turned, walking slowly towards the door. He reached for the knob, but for just a second, his fingers hovered over it without touching. A breath. A pause - the metal clicked softly, and the door latched shut behind him.
 
I stayed sitting in my chair for a little longer, watching the sunset dancing on the wall opposite the window. Red, orange, yellow, flitting over the aged mahogany. 
The shifting light caught the edges of my desk, and suddenly, I wasn’t in the room anymore - I was back in the library, years ago, the day I found the letter, folded neatly and carefully hidden behind The Art of Diplomacy, Volume II. That morning, I had been filled with a quiet kind of pride - poring over my spotted reflection in the mirror, running my fingers over the barely-present wisps of blond on my chin. The first step, Father had said, to becoming a man.
Amadeus Dain, my father’s royal butler, had a son - an unusual thing for a man in his position. But my father, ever the saint, made exceptions for those he valued. He let young Milan live with Amadeus in the butler’s quarters, and allowed us children to play together in the gardens - my absolute dream as an only child. Milan was free only while he wasn’t shadowing his father, which gave him most hours outside meals and evenings free.
So, Milan and I had spent that afternoon running around the palace grounds, daring each other to climb to the top of tall trees and scale forbidden statues in the sprawling gardens. By evening, covered in mud and exhausted, we went back to our quarters to change out of our muddied clothes. With Milan still in his room and Father locked away in an evening Council meeting, I wandered to the royal library, searching for something - anything - to pass the time. 
After deciding, intellectually I might add, on Art of Diplomacy, I went to pull the book off the shelf. But as I did so, a flash of white paper fell and floaated to the ground. I balanced the book on the shelf with my left hand, and leant down to reach the paper. The folds were pressed tightly, and almost ironed at the edges - as if the author had never intended it to be read.
Gently unfolding it, I began to read, written in beautiful red cursive:


If I were the winds of Ceranciz 
I would linger, caught in the spaces 
You leave behind

If I were the sun of Pulma 
I would burn softer, only
To touch, without daring 
To claim

But I am nothing
Only shadow, only silence
Only a name never meant to be spoken
Beside yours

Yet I dream of two birds, uncaged
One from the gold, one from the plains
And to meet on the silent, white peaks
Beyond crowns, beyond shadows, beyond names

The book nearly slipped from my grasp. I let it fall back onto the shelf, my focus entirely on the slip of parchment in my hands. I read it once. Then twice. By the third time, I was whispering the words relentlessly under my breath as if they might change their meaning, a catechism of a faith I hadn’t known I possessed.
I would linger, caught in the spaces you leave behind. 
I stood there for what felt like hours, the oddly comforting scent of old paper and leather surrounding me, the poem a flickering light in the dim library. 
Only to touch, without daring to claim. 
I didn’t need to question who had written it. Only one other soul frequented these shelves with the same reverence I did.
Only a name never meant to be spoken beside yours.
I found him later that night in my childish fervour, not with an accusation, but with a question. He was skipping stones across the lake, and I waited until his pockets were empty. Of course, he fiercely denied he wrote it. He had to.
He tried to laugh it off, a brittle, hollow sound that was so unlike him. Young Remus wasn’t satisfied. He claimed it was a joke, a mistake, a verse copied from some forgotten book. But then I saw it - the slight tightness in his breath, the fear in his eyes. My voice softened. I never wanted my friend, my good friend, to feel like that. “Milan.”
And just like that, the charade crumbled. He fell into my arms, his body wracked with silent, desperate sobs. I will always remember holding him close to my chest, my linen shirt dampening from his tears, silent sobbing from the premature revelation of a secret a lifetime in the making. I remember comforting him, telling him everything would be okay. I remember that unfamiliar, warm feeling while hugging him, that somehow my comforting him soothed something within me too. I don’t remember deciding to kiss him. Only the salt of his tears on his cheek, the way he tensed for a heartbeat then softened, his breath shaky but steadying. I don’t remember why, just that it felt right. Whatever affection I felt for Milan overpowered any other sensibilities I had at the time. We stayed by the lakeside for hours until the sun went down and I knew that Father would come looking for us.
Everything and nothing changed after that day; we still ran through the gardens, climbed statues we shouldn't have, collapsed in the grass, breathless with laughter. But Milan carried himself differently - shoulders looser, steps tighter, smiles wider. We grew closer in ways I couldn’t quite name then, and my feet found their way to his door more evenings than not. The palace was vast, but somehow, he was always within reach.
Then came the slow, inevitable march of death and duty .I remember the day he first appeared in his father’s livery, the formal suit of the royal butler. The boy I knew was gone, replaced by a man who stood at attention, hands folded neatly behind his back, belonging to someone else. As my father’s health waned, so did too the time Milan and I had left, for stolen moments. He was always at my father’s side, just beyond my reach. 
And then, one day, my father was gone too - and Milan was beside me instead. In the years that followed, Milan became something more than a butler, more than an adviser - something unnamed, something unprecedented. It was his voice, patient and unwavering, that had guided me towards this moment. The decree was his dream as much as mine. And now it was done. The ink had dried. The gavel had struck the wood. The weight should have lifted. So why did I still feel so lost?
The memory slipped from my grasp, the present settling back like a weight on my shoulders as I blinked, suddenly aware of the darkness around me. The warm colours had long since faded from the mahogany wall, and only cold shadows remained.

The week ahead loomed, tiring and unrelenting. Tomorrow, the Royal Council. I would be addressing the people of Sinj on Wednesday morning. The day after, the House of Nobles. And then, Friday, Saturday, and Sunday, the true ordeal -  the annual Assembly of the International Alliance of Nations. Three days of manoeuvring, smiling through clenched teeth, trying to stop the country from collapsing underneath my feet. 
This year’s Assembly was set to stoke tensions high - Alandir would be hot on my case over their claims to the Bistrica mines, their patience thinning with every passing year. And there was always Boscovia, circling the Moravice issue like vultures waiting for a body to drop. I could only hope I'd curried enough favour within the international community to support me against their case. If House Mannford didn't fly into a fury over what I'd just done, I could certainly count on the backing of Kruhlstutt. If. I wondered if I had chosen the worst timing after all. 
With all these thoughts swimming in my head, it was time to sleep. As if on cue, Gustav gave a sharp rap on the door. I stood up as the door opened, and he bowed - deep, precise, practiced. Without saying a word, he stood aside to let me pass. As I walked back to my quarters, he fell in behind me, a shadow in lockstep, like he always was. 












The Remus Decree
A firm knock on the door. 
“Come in, Councillor.”
The heavy oak door to the Council Room swung open, revealing a tall, stately woman. Councillor Karela Toran stepped inside, her hand clasped firmly around a navy briefcase. 
Councillor Karela Toran had been serving on the Royal Council longer than I had been alive. Once a high-profile and formidable member of the House of Nobles, and of course, my father’s cousin. She soon became a trusted adviser and confidant of his, and he gave her an official title as Councillor of Affairs of the Kingdom not two years into his rule. She has dutifully served in that position ever since.
She offered me a precise, clipped bow, her tightly curled grey hair bobbing gently as she did so, and stepped without hesitation further into the room. 
“Good morning, Your Majesty”, she said as she pulled out her chair and settled into it with practiced ease. 
“Good morning, Karela,” I replied, offering a respectful nod in return. 
The gilded hands of the wall clock pointed to nine o'clock precisely. On the dot, as always.
Another knock at the door. 
“Come in.”
Councillor Vihrdecker marched through the door, his boots striking the wooden floor with authority, in sharp contrast to the measured steps of Councillors Calehart and Uval filing in just after him. 
I never quite felt at ease around Vihrdecker - Lyssa always joked with me that he must have been born seven foot tall. His hair was thick and greyed, and a rigorously-trimmed moustache and beard framed his chin, each hair standing to attention. His commanding presence was only heightened by his imposing traditional Kresimirian dark red jacket, which bore a polished array of medals - complete with the gleaming Golden Deer on his chest, the highest honour achievable in the Kingdom of Kresimiria. 
As if the sea of medals left any doubt in the mind, General Alfrek Vihrdecker served as my Councillor of War. He had clawed his way through the ranks, all the way from an officer to General of the Kresimirian Divine Army. For over forty years, he had shaped battlefields and commanded tens of thousands of men - until I convinced him to trade the war room for the Council Chamber. Even now, seated, he never seemed at rest. A man built for command, like him, rarely is.

Councillor Jens Calehart settled in beside Vihrdecker. He shrugged his dark blazer onto the back of his chair, revealing a freshly-pressed white shirt and a bold blue tie. Calehart always had the air of a man with a two-hundred-page contract waiting in his briefcase - one convoluted, disingenuously crafted, and with enough loopholes to make the Archbishop swear. This, of course, was precisely why I had chosen him as my Councillor of Foreign Affairs. With all the suffering Kresimiria had endured due to unfavourable treaties and contracts over the years, I was determined to restore the Kingdom to its rightful place on the international stage - and I needed the best negotiator the Kingdom could provide to accomplish this. 
Calehart was admittedly an unorthodox Council appointee - he had no relation whatsoever to any noble houses, nor was he particularly wizened with experience. Not yet even thirty, and still unable to grow so much as a shadow of stubble, he had earned the unfortunate nickname of ‘babyface’ behind the closed doors of many Kresimirian noble households. He nonetheless endured it, with the patience of a man with more ambition than experience, who knew he would outwit them all in time.
He had recently graduated from Sinj University, much as I did several years ago - though where I studied history, he mastered the art of rhetoric warfare. He had first appeared on my radar when he had several impressive successes  in debates at the Sinj Institute for Oracy. I found he had a certain way of skillfully manipulating words that made you question your own sanity and resignedly admit defeat. When I saw him reduce Professor Ivan Matar to shocked silence as he tore apart his textbook’s interpretation of the Constitution of Kresimiria, listing countless mistakes, I knew I would need Calehart in my Council. 

	Councillor Tiras Uval took the seat on Calehart's left, adjusting his red tie - emblazoned, of course, with the soaring white falcon of House Uval, as if anyone could possibly forget. As he placed his folder onto the desk, he ran his weathered fingers exasperatedly through his long sandy hair. My Councillor for Industry, he had made his name decades ago by funneling House Uval's wealth into the mining business - to great success, multiplying their riches more than tenfold. His family's legacy, the Uval Gold Company, was more than a business; it was a dynasty. Nearly three-quarters of Kresimiria’s mines lay in Uval hands, including our crown jewels - the highly prosperous northern Bistrica Gold Mines. 

“Good morning Alfrek, Jens, Tiras,” I said.
As the men made their various greetings to me, there was a final knock at the door. 
“Enter,” I said, and she did - each movement graceful, but deliberate: Councillor Amel Esquilda. A cascading violet dress wrapped around her legs, and her long blonde hair flowed in equal weight over her shoulders. She gently bobbed a curtsy as she entered, and made her way to the final seat at the table. Her looks nevertheless deceiving, Esquilda was a sharp and prescriptive economist - and a very useful ally to have on the Council. Empathetic, kind, and forward-thinking, she was often the one to speak up the loudest for those who couldn’t. However, her steadfast support of wealth redistribution and equality in the Kingdom had many a time provoked the attention, and often ire, of the noble houses - especially the affluent House Uval. Where there was wealth, there were Uvals. And where there were Uvals, there would be riches to be made; and debt to be paid.

My five Councillors settled around the table, light chatter and amicable greetings between them.  The Council Room was brightly lit thanks to the morning spring sun through the wide windows, and a gentle breeze was slowly clearing the wintry dust that had gathered in all corners of the room during the colder months.
“Thank you all for arriving here promptly”, I began. “We have much to discuss. Amel, let’s begin with your report.”
Councillor Esquilda nodded, and glanced quickly at her notes. 
“Well, Your Majesty, would you like the good news or the bad news first?” She didn’t wait for my answer before continuing. “The good news is, the Bistrica Gold Mines hit record productivity last week. Seven thousand troy ounces.” She glanced over at Tiras, who, indeed, had a smug satisfaction about him. 
“However, the bad news”, she said, stressing the word, “is the way this was achieved. Unemployment in Bistrica City and the surrounding Lipovljana country has reached forty nine percent, Your Majesty.”  
Murmurs spread around the table, and I stared at her in confusion. “How?” I said. “I thought the mines brought in thousands of jobs?”
Esquilda sighed. “They did, Your Majesty. But as of two weeks ago, the mines are no longer hiring local workers. The workforce policy has been adjusted to almost exclusively hire Valkari immigrants.”
I looked over at Tiras, whose face expressed no embarrassment nor regret, leaving only a slight smirk.
“What’s more,” she continued, “not only are these immigrants paid insultingly low sums for their work, under the table - as non-citizens, they’re technically not even covered under the Mining Safety Act!” She frowned and slammed her hand firmly on her papers, clearly mortified at the state of the Kingdom’s laws. “This isn’t sustainable, Your Majesty. The last thing you want, especially now, is any semblance of strikes in our biggest northern city.”
“That’s preposterous.” Tiras leant forward with his hands clasped on the table. His strong, Western drawl filled the room with the cloying scent of Topusko pears trees. 
“The immigrants won’t strike. They can’t, it’s forbidden by their contract.” I saw Jens exchange a brief cynical glance with Esquilda before looking away - even he hadn’t expected Tiras to be that blatant. “If they strike, they’re gone,” Tiras continued obliviously. “Immigrants are spilling over from Valkaristan every week, Remus, it’s only logical that Uval Gold makes the most of that opportunity. We gave them jobs, Your Majesty.”
He looked over to me, pointing his finger in my direction.
“They’re feeding their families. They’re paying their rent. If anything, Uval Gold has given them more than their homeland ever could.”
Esquilda looked disgusted, but she held her tongue, instead watching me expectantly. Uval Gold, regrettably, played a significant part in holding up the Kresimirian economy. Our gold exports kept us a relevant player on the international stage, and the Bistrica Mines were a key cornerstone of my father’s legacy of noble unity. I hated to deny Esquilda the deserved moral victory in this, but I needed to sugarcoat my anger towards Uval - the House of Nobles could descend into chaos at his very word.  
“Tiras, be reasonable,” I said, trying not to sound tired. “We can’t have thousands of Kresimirians laid off.”
He was quick to counter. “Don’t worry, Your Majesty,” he said, with the suggestion of a twinkle in his eye. “Uval Gold is opening four new steelworks in the North - and the board has agreed that they’ll be hiring exclusively Kresimirians.”
“Citizens, that is,” he added, nodding in the direction of Councillor Esquilda, drumming his fingers mockingly (rhythmically?) on the polished surface of the Council table.
I sighed. “Good. I want to see employment rise in Lipovljana, Tiras. Not fall even more.” I stared at him a moment longer, as if to make sure he heard me. 
“For now, let’s move on. Esquilda. Anything else?”
“Yes, Your Majesty,” she said. “Inflation, especially in the West, is rising at rapid rates. In border cities like Varazdinske, food has hit unaffordable prices. Citizens are struggling to feed their families, and markets are beginning to report empty stocks.”
Tiras scoffed. “And? Don’t you think you have anything to do with that?  You and your ‘Rebuilding Initiative’?” 
The Initiative had started as what seemed a miracle, but had ended as a warning to future generations of economists. In the South, people called Esquilda a saint, the profits reinvested in their cities but across the country, especially in the West, they still called Esquilda a fool and a tyrant. I was inclined to agree with them in an objective sense - thousands left jobless after markets collapsed. The country was only saved from chaos when Uval Gold stepped in to provide buffer wages until the economy recovered - I hated to accept handouts, especially from the Uvals, where they functioned more as a blood debt - but we had no choice. 
“Not to mention those Valkari in the West”, Tiras continued. I saw Jens wince at the mention of this, while Karela nodded vehemently, and spoke up from her seat on my left.
“Councillor Uval has a point. I paid a visit to Varazdinske not long ago, Your Majesty. I saw boarded up shop windows, streets littered with debris, and Valkaz graffiti everywhere. The Valkari are running the western cities, Your Majesty. What people are saying is true. Not satisfied with destroying their own country in civil war, they've come to tear down ours too.”
Esquilda shook her head vigorously. “Don't you have an ounce of compassion, Councillor Toran? Valkari immigrants suffer from immense racial hatred and discrimination in our country, especially in the West. Besides, the vast majority are civilian refugees, women and children, fleeing the senseless violence that Tarkeva* is fueling.”
“Oh, nonsense,” Karela snapped. She tapped her fingers against the table, her lips pressed into a thin line. “My apologies for the dismissive tone, Councillor Esquilda, but if you’d seen what I’d seen, you wouldn’t have the slightest bit of empathy for those brutes.” Karela straightened her posture, and glanced back at the paper on the table.
Oksana Tarkeva, President of the Republic of Valkaristan. She initiated a radical program of nationalisation and foreign isolationism. During her harsh crackdown on liberal voices in the country, the Valkari Liberation Army, led by Adam Barecz, organised and took over most of the West of the country. Violent fighting taking place on both sides, and there is a massive refugee crisis. Kruhlstut closed its borders (part of the deal maybe?) and so Kresimiria is one of few where they still come**. 
Esquilda sighed resignedly. 
“The point is, Your Majesty, that a nationwide food shortage is not far off the horizon. Alandir have been cutting their meat exports to us year on year ever since Uval Gold broke off their contract with Alandir Mining Corp.”
The room glanced at Uval, whose face, as usual, betrayed no emotions other than smug, unembarrassed satisfaction. 
“Oh, and,” she said, turning a page in her folder, “right.” She shook her hair out of her face. “General Vihrdecker, you might know more about this.”
