Someone I was seeing once replied to a long, carefully worded message with a single word: *Fine.*

Not *Fine!* Not *That's fine x*. Just *Fine.* with a full stop.

I read it on the bus, somewhere between a lecture I was late for and a supermarket I didn't need anything from. My chest tightened. I re-read our last twelve messages. I checked whether they'd been online. I drafted three replies, deleted all of them, and sent something breezy that I didn't mean: *Haha no worries!*

They were probably just tired. I was probably overthinking it. Both things were true, and only one of them was my problem to fix.

That was years ago now, with someone I'll call **M**. I'm in a different relationship today — with someone I'll call **A** — and the same situation would barely register. Not because I've become cold, or because A is magically easier to read, but because I've slowly, painfully, embarrassingly slowly, moved from what people call **anxious attachment** toward something closer to **secure**.

This isn't a therapy blog post, and I'm not qualified to diagnose anybody. But if you've ever turned a read receipt into a whole personality assessment, this might resonate.

## Attachment in plain English

Attachment theory started with how babies relate to caregivers, but most adults recognise the patterns in romance and close friendship too. The simplified version:

- **Secure:** You can want closeness without panicking when it's not immediate. You assume good intent until shown otherwise.
- **Anxious:** You crave reassurance. Distance feels like rejection. You monitor the emotional temperature of the relationship constantly.
- **Avoidant:** Closeness feels suffocating. You pull away when things get serious, even if you like the person.
- **Disorganised:** A mix — you want intimacy and fear it at the same time. (Often tied to harder early experiences; I'm not going to pretend I understand this one from the inside.)

Nobody is one box forever. Stress, sleep deprivation, a bad week at work, and suddenly you're seventeen again, refreshing a chat window.

The useful part isn't the label. It's noticing the *loop*.

## My anxious era

I didn't hear the phrase "anxious attachment" until university. Before that I just thought I was **intense**.

I fell hard and fast. I remembered small details people mentioned once, three months earlier, because my brain treated affection like exam revision — if I could prove I'd been listening, maybe they'd stay. I confused *caring a lot* with *caring well*.

With M, the pattern was familiar in hindsight. They were kind in person and vague over text. Plans would shift. I'd fill the gaps myself.

> *They're busy. They're stressed. They still like me.*

> *They're losing interest. I did something wrong. I should ask again.*

Both playlists ran at once. The anxious brain doesn't pick one; it shuffles.

I wrote about lossy compression [in another post](/blog/language-as-lossy-compression) — how language strips tone and context, and the listener rebuilds the message with their own biases. Anxious attachment is what happens when **your rebuild algorithm always assumes the worst**. A delayed reply isn't "they're in a seminar." It's "they're bored of me." A short answer isn't "they're tired." It's "they're angry."

M wasn't a villain. I don't think they were avoidant on purpose, or playing games. We were young, bad at naming needs, and worse at timing. I wanted more reassurance than they knew how to give; they wanted more space than I knew how to offer. The mismatch hurt both of us, in different ways.

There were good moments — long walks, stupid in-jokes, the feeling of being chosen. But I was always scanning for when it would end. That's an exhausting way to love someone.

## What secure actually looks like

Secure attachment is not "never worrying." It's not becoming the cool, detached person who takes three days to reply because they "don't do phones."

It's closer to this: **you can feel worried, and not let the worry drive the car.**

With A, I still notice the old reflexes. A message left on read for an hour? A flicker. A change of plans? A flicker. The difference is what happens next.

Old me: spiral, draft a passive-aggressive paragraph, send a meme instead, hate myself for sending the meme.

Current me (most of the time): *Hmm. I feel a bit wobbly. I'll ask, or I'll wait, or I'll go for a walk and see if it's still wobbling in an hour.*

A doesn't perform constant reassurance. They also don't disappear for days and call it mystique. They say when they're busy. They say when something's wrong. When I say "I'm feeling a bit insecure about us — can we talk later?", they don't treat it like a lawsuit.

That last sentence took years to learn how to type.

Secure relationships, in my limited experience, are **a little bit boring in the best way**. Not fireworks every Tuesday. More like: we said we'd call at eight, and we called at eight, and nobody had to decode anything.

I wrote once about [turning my phone greyscale](/blog/black-and-white-phone) to make scrolling less addictive. Attachment work felt similar — reducing the neon pull of notifications, so a red badge wasn't the most important thing in the room.

## How you move the needle (slowly)

I didn't wake up secure. I don't think I'm fully there now. But a few things actually helped, beyond "just communicate better" (the most annoying advice in the world, because anxious people are often *over*-communicating).

### 1. Naming the pattern without naming the person

Reading about attachment gave me vocabulary that wasn't "I'm too much" or "they're cold." It was: *my system is threat-detecting.* That sounds clinical, but it created distance. The feeling is real; the catastrophe isn't always.

### 2. Delaying the send

If a message is really about reassurance — *do you still like me? are we okay?* — I try to wait. Not to play games. Because the version I write at peak anxiety is rarely the version I mean at peak calm. Sometimes I still send it. But the pause helps.

### 3. Building a life that isn't one person

This sounds obvious and is annoyingly hard as a student who moves cities, makes friends in bursts, and spends too much time online. But projects, mates, walks, hobbies — anything that reminds your nervous system you exist outside one chat thread — genuinely lowers the stakes.

### 4. Choosing differently (without blaming the past)

I'm not saying "just date secure people" as if that's a personality you order online. But I am saying: **repeated dynamics teach you what feels normal.** If chaos feels like passion, calm can feel like indifference until you recalibrate.

A and I argue sometimes. We misunderstand each other. The difference is the repair — we come back, we explain, we don't leave each other guessing for sport.

### 5. Accepting that some stories just end

M and I didn't have a cinematic breakup or a lesson neatly delivered. We drifted, mismatched, and it hurt. I spent a while convinced that if I'd been cooler, less needy, more mysterious, it would have worked. Maybe. Probably not. Wrong people aren't homework.

## Anxious isn't broken

I want to be careful here. Anxious attachment gets pathologised, especially for women and queer people and anyone who's been told they're "too emotional." Caring deeply isn't a character flaw. The problem is when caring becomes **self-abandonment** — when you shrink, chase, or perform to keep someone who won't meet you halfway.

Moving toward secure, for me, meant learning that love isn't supposed to feel like waiting for a grade you can never see.

## Still learning

Last month A went quiet for an evening — phone dead, out with friends, no drama intended. Old instincts fired: *What did I do?* I didn't send four messages. I watched a film. They texted when they were home: *Sorry, battery died. Love you.*

I cried a little, which is embarrassing to admit, not from sadness but from relief that the new pathway exists.

I'm not writing this because I've solved relationships. I'm writing it because nobody told twenty-year-old me that the goal wasn't to need less — it was to **need safely**. To ask without begging. To leave when you're not being met. To stay when you are.

If you're in the *Fine.* phase of something right now: you're probably not crazy. Your nervous system might just be running an old program. You can update it. It takes ages. It doesn't require becoming someone colder — just someone a little kinder to yourself while the message sends.
