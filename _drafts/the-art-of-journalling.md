I have been using Daylio for somewhere between seven and eight years now. I say *somewhere* because the beginning of any long streak is always a little fuzzy. There are early entries that are probably less a record of a life than evidence that I had downloaded an app and was pleased with myself for using it.

But the streak is there. Thousands of tiny records: a mood, a few activities, occasionally a sentence, occasionally several paragraphs written far too late at night. It has outlasted school, moving house, university, friendships, versions of myself I can now recognise only by the things they were worried about.

That is an absurd amount of life to leave in an app designed around tapping a smiling face.

## The smallness is the point

When people talk about journalling, they often mean the beautiful version: a leather notebook, a fountain pen, a candle doing unnecessary work in the corner of the room. There is nothing wrong with that. I love that image. But it is intimidating, and it quietly suggests that a day has to deserve a page before it can be recorded.

Most days do not feel as if they deserve a page.

Most days are: woke up late. Went for a walk. Played something. Felt a little strange. Watched a film. Called someone. Did laundry. Had an argument. Didn't have an argument. Ate pasta. Thought about sending a message and did not.

The great trick of Daylio, at least for me, is that it makes the minimum viable journal almost embarrassingly easy. You can log a day badly. You can log it when you are tired. You can log it in twenty seconds. You can put “bored”, “home”, and “gaming” down and move on.

This is not a failure of the journal. It is its survival mechanism.

The best habit is not the most beautiful one. It is the one that survives the boring Tuesday in February when you have nothing insightful to say.

## You cannot remember the texture of a year

Memory is very good at preserving peaks. It remembers the first day somewhere new, the last day somewhere old, the embarrassing thing you said at a party, the holiday, the heartbreak, the big result, the person you were obsessed with for six months.

It is much worse at preserving texture.

What was I actually doing in July? Which friends was I seeing most? How often did I leave the house? Was I really miserable all the time, or did I just remember the bad bits with better cinematography? When did I start writing that project? When did I stop?

The journal cannot answer every question. It is still only a set of notes written by me, with all the blind spots that implies. But it gives memory something to argue with.

I can go back and find a day that I had completely forgotten. A small trip. A dog walk. A particular song playing in the car. A silly joke with somebody who is no longer in my life. The entry might only be two lines, but those two lines can reopen an entire room in my head.

That is the strange power of keeping notes: the information is tiny, but the retrieval is enormous.

## Moods are not grades

There is a danger with mood tracking. Once you have a graph, it becomes tempting to treat your own life as a performance review. Too many bad days in a month and you start to feel as though you have failed at being a person. A week of “good” moods looks like progress; a week of “tired” moods looks like a regression.

I do not think that is what the data is for.

The mood is not a verdict. It is a weather report.

Sometimes the weather is genuinely bad. Sometimes you are just tired. Sometimes you log “okay” because nothing particularly happened, and that can be a gift. A flat line is not always emptiness; sometimes it is peace, routine, safety, the absence of catastrophe.

Over seven or eight years, the entries have made one thing very clear to me: feelings are much less permanent than they feel from inside them. You can have a terrible evening and then a completely ordinary morning. You can be lonely while being surrounded by people. You can be happy on a day that looks unremarkable in retrospect. The record does not solve any of this, but it makes it harder to believe that a feeling is the whole story.

## The journal as a witness

There are entries I would not show anyone. Not because they are scandalous, necessarily, but because they are unfinished. They contain an opinion I no longer hold, a fear I no longer have, or a version of an event that I would now describe differently.

That is exactly why they are worth keeping.

A journal should not be a polished autobiography written by someone who already knows what everything meant. It should be allowed to contain wrong first drafts of your own life.

There is something quietly comforting about that. The person writing an entry at seventeen did not know who they would be at twenty-three. They could not know which friendships would endure, which plans would become jokes, or which disasters would turn out to be survivable. They were doing their best with the information available.

So was I. So am I now.

The point of returning to old entries is not to mock the younger version of yourself for being dramatic. It is to offer them the generosity of context. Of course they were worried. Of course it felt enormous. They had not reached the next page yet.

## A record, not an obligation

I have not kept the streak perfectly. There are sparse days, repeated activities, entries written from memory the next morning, little stretches where I clearly did not want to talk to myself very much. That is fine.

The journal is not a moral test. It is not proof that I have been productive, emotionally mature, or interesting. It is just a record of having been here.

That might be the art of journalling, if it can be called an art: noticing that the ordinary day is not empty simply because it does not make a story yet.

The story comes later. Years later, sometimes. When you scroll back through a sequence of small notes and realise that, without announcing itself, your life was changing all along.
