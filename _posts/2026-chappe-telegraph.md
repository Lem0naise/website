Claude Chappe first demonstrated his new invention, the 'optical telegraph', in 1791. He proposed towers with a large rotating 'regulator' bar with two smaller 'indicator' arms. Each of the 92 usable positions of these arms mapped to a page in a codebook, which had 92 symbols per page - allowing 8,464 possible words. 
By 1845, the telegraph network spanned over 5,000km with over 500 stations, allowing messages to travel over 500 km/h.

However, there was a vulnerability - Chappe created a control signal to cancel the last transmitted sign; essentially a backspace key. The telegraph operator relayed every signal they saw immediately, and corrections would be applied later using the backspace signal.

In come the Blanc brothers, Joseph and Francois, French stockbrockers who worked in the southern city of Bordeaux. They bribed a telegraph operator, instructing the operator to insert a fake signal symbolising the movement of the Paris stock market that day, followed immediately by the 'backspace' signal into official government messages. This signal would travel across the entire telegraph network down to the Blanc brothers, but because of the backspace, would not show in the eventual message, thus acting as a hidden signal. Front-running the stock market over two years netted the brothers aroundd 100,000 francs.

Despite evidence of over 121 instances of the signal being used, the brothers did not face prison time, as there was no law against abusing the telegraph system in that way! Immediately after the trial, the French government natoinalised all telegraph neteworks, and banned transmitting unauthorised signals, the first cyber-defence law.

The records confirm the core facts of the case:

    The Scheme: Between August 1834 and August 1836, the Blanc brothers corrupted a telegraph official in Tours. A Paris correspondent sent parcels containing gloves or stockings; the colour indicated whether the 3% government bond had risen or fallen. The official then inserted a pre-arranged false signal into the telegraph line, which was read in Bordeaux and passed to the Blancs.

    The Volume: The prosecution established that the brothers received this fraudulent signal 121 times over the two-year period.

    The Outcome: The jury found that the official had indeed transmitted non-official signals and received payments, but it also found he had not done so "in the exercise of his official employment." This technicality meant the corruption charges could not stand. The Blanc brothers were absolved (not strictly acquitted) and ordered to pay the costs of the trial.
